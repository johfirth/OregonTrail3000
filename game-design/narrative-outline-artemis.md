# Narrative Outline — Lunar Colony 3000

> **Status**: Draft
> **Author**: Game Design Team
> **Last Updated**: 2025-07-18
> **Parent Document**: [Lunar Colony Overview](lunar-colony-overview.md)

## Overview

Lunar Colony 3000 tells the story of Artemis VII — humanity's most ambitious crewed mission. The player is the Mission Commander, leading four specialists from Kennedy Space Center across 240,000 miles of vacuum to establish the first permanent lunar colony at the Moon's south pole. The narrative is delivered in second-person present tense, weaving between tense mission narration, crew dialogue, and darkly humorous commentary in the Oregon Trail tradition. Every milestone is a chance to remind the player: you are very far from home, you are running out of everything, and the Moon does not care.

---

## Theme & Tone

### Central Theme

**Humanity's drive to explore and settle new frontiers.**

The same stubborn, reckless, magnificent impulse that drove pioneers into covered wagons and pointed them at the Rocky Mountains now drives us to strap ourselves to rockets and aim for the Moon. Oregon Trail's pioneers left Independence, Missouri, with everything they owned in a wagon, knowing they might not survive the journey. Your crew leaves Kennedy Space Center in a spacecraft, knowing the same. The frontier has changed. The spirit hasn't.

Lunar Colony 3000 is about the cost of exploration — measured in fuel, in food, in the lives of the people who volunteer to go first. But it's also about what we gain: the knowledge that we can, the proof that we did, and the stubborn, irrefutable fact that humanity refuses to stay put.

### Tone

- **Primary tone**: Tense but hopeful, with dark humor in the Oregon Trail tradition
- **Tonal range**: The tone shifts deliberately across the mission arc:
  - **Wonder and excitement** (launch, first Earth views from orbit) — awe at the scale of what we're doing
  - **Anxiety and watchfulness** (lunar transit) — the quiet dread of monitoring systems in a tin can surrounded by vacuum
  - **Strategic focus** (Gateway station) — the shopkeeper's calculations, the gambler's odds
  - **White-knuckle tension** (powered descent) — everything depends on the next sixty seconds
  - **Gritty determination** (surface operations) — pioneer endurance, one EVA at a time
  - **Triumph or tragedy** (colony establishment) — the final reckoning
- **Humor**: Dark, understated, and character-driven. Humor appears in crew banter, Mission Control quips, and the absurd specificity of space procedure. It is never mean-spirited. The game laughs with you, not at you — except when you die, and then it laughs gently, in the grand Oregon Trail tradition.
- **Gravity**: Death is treated with a beat of real silence before the game moves on. Crew losses are brief but felt. The game doesn't dwell, but it doesn't flinch either.

### Historical Context

- **Real history**: Grounded in NASA's Artemis program (2024–2030) — real rockets, real landing sites, real technology. The Space Launch System, Lunar Gateway, Human Landing System, and ISRU processors are all based on actual hardware in development or testing.
- **Accuracy vs. drama**: Orbital mechanics are simplified for gameplay (turns instead of continuous trajectory), but the *feel* is authentic — the fear of a solar flare, the cost of a course correction, the weight of every kilogram. We dramatize the experience, not the technology.
- **Perspectives**: The crew is international and diverse. The mission is a human achievement, not an American one — though it launches from American soil and is funded by American taxpayers, which creates its own narrative tension (Congressional oversight, public pressure, media attention).
- **Oregon Trail parallel**: Just as Oregon Trail captured a real historical moment — the Great Migration of the 1840s — Lunar Colony 3000 captures a moment that is about to happen. Players fifty years from now should look back at this game the way we look back at Oregon Trail: "That's roughly how it felt."

## Story Structure

### Act Structure

The narrative follows a three-act structure mapped across the game's 7 phases.

---

#### Act 1: "Leaving Home" (Phases 1–2: Mission Prep & Launch)

- **Setting**: Kennedy Space Center, Merritt Island, Florida. The Vehicle Assembly Building. Pad 39B. Low Earth Orbit.
- **Setup**: The player meets their crew, receives the mission briefing, allocates resources, and confronts the zero-sum reality of spaceflight: you can't take everything, and everything you don't take might kill you. The world is watching. The President is watching. Your crew is watching you.
- **Inciting incident**: The countdown begins. T-minus ten. The solid rocket boosters ignite. The hold-down bolts blow. There is no turning back — you are committed to the Moon or to a very expensive firework.
- **Player goal**: Get off the ground alive. Get into orbit. Make the Go/No-Go call for Trans-Lunar Injection. Leave Earth behind.
- **Emotional beat**: Excitement layered over anxiety. The thrill of launch, the vertigo of orbit, the dawning realization that Earth is now below you, not around you. The crew is optimistic but wired. The commander is in charge — and alone with the weight of that.

#### Act 2: "The Crossing" (Phases 3–5: Transit, Gateway, Descent)

- **Rising action**: The transit is quiet and unforgiving. Resources drain. Random events test the crew. A solar flare forces a shelter decision. A micrometeorite punches through outer shielding. A crew member can't sleep. The void presses in. Each turn is a small survival story, and the player watches their margins shrink.
- **Midpoint**: Arriving at Lunar Gateway. The Moon fills the viewport. For the first time, the destination is *real* — not an abstract number on a trajectory plot, but a grey, cratered world hanging in front of you. Gateway is the last fort before the wilderness. The player resupplies (if they can afford to), selects a landing site (the single biggest strategic decision in the game), and transfers to the lander.
- **Complications**: The landing site selection creates a committed path — each site has unique risks and rewards. The powered descent is the difficulty spike: fuel management, terrain hazards, abort decisions. This is the mountain pass. Everything the player did in Phases 1–4 pays off or pays out here.
- **Character development**: Crew bonds form through shared hardship during transit. Chen's calm becomes an anchor. Rivera's anxiety becomes endearing. Okafor's enthusiasm becomes essential for morale. Park's dark humor becomes the crew's coping mechanism. By Gateway, these aren't just gameplay bonuses — they're people.

#### Act 3: "New World" (Phases 6–7: Surface Operations & Colony Establishment)

- **Climax**: The first EVA. Stepping onto the lunar surface for the first time. The habitat deployment. The ice extraction missions into permanently shadowed craters — the darkest, coldest places any human has ever stood. Equipment fails. Dust contaminates everything. A moonquake shakes the habitat. The crew must survive long enough to build something permanent.
- **Resolution**: Colony assessment. Did you bring enough? Did enough of your crew survive? Is the habitat pressurized? Is the ice flowing? The game tallies the score and delivers one of five endings — from "Thriving Colony" to "Total Mission Loss."
- **Denouement**: A final message to Earth. The commander records a transmission. What they say depends on what happened. If the colony stands, it's triumph. If the crew is diminished, it's bittersweet. If only the commander remains, it's a quiet, private grief broadcast to eight billion people.

## Opening Narration

The first text the player reads. This sets the tone for the entire game.

> The year is 2028.
>
> Three years ago, NASA planted the first boots on the Moon's south pole since the Apollo program ended. That mission lasted seventy-two hours. Yours is meant to last forever.
>
> You are the Mission Commander of Artemis VII — the most ambitious crewed spaceflight in human history. Your orders are simple to state and almost impossible to execute: fly a crew of five to the lunar south pole, land safely, and establish the first permanent human colony beyond Earth.
>
> Your crew is the best NASA has. Dr. Alex Chen, your pilot — cool, precise, and quietly brilliant. Specialist Jordan Rivera, your flight engineer — meticulous to the point of superstition, and worth every checklist. Dr. Sam Okafor, your mission scientist — the person who mapped the ice deposits you're going to mine, and who has waited their entire career for this flight. Dr. Morgan Park, your medical officer — three missions, zero crew losses, and a sense of humor dark enough to survive the vacuum.
>
> They're counting on you. So is Houston. So is the President. So are eight billion people watching the livestream.
>
> You stand in the Vehicle Assembly Building at Kennedy Space Center, looking up at the Space Launch System rocket — 322 feet of liquid hydrogen, solid propellant, and human ambition. In forty-eight hours, you'll be sitting on top of it. In four days, you'll be farther from home than any human has ever been. In two weeks, you'll either be building a colony on the Moon or you'll be dead.
>
> Your mission budget is fixed. Your crew is irreplaceable. The Moon doesn't negotiate, and space doesn't forgive.
>
> Choose wisely. Pack carefully. Fly well.
>
> And whatever you do — don't run out of oxygen.

## Branching Paths

### Major Decision Points

#### Landing Site Selection (Phase 4)

- **When**: After docking with Lunar Gateway, before committing to descent
- **Context**: The player has survived transit and resupplied (or hasn't). Now they must choose where to land. This is the most consequential strategic decision in the game.
- **Options**:
  1. **Shackleton Crater Rim** → Safest landing, best solar power, moderate ice. The conservative play.
  2. **de Gerlache Crater Rim** → Rougher terrain, excellent ice access, moderate everything else. The ice-focused play.
  3. **Nobile Crater Rim** → Extremely rough terrain, richest ice deposits, scarce power. The expert's gamble.
  4. **Connecting Ridge** → Balanced across all attributes. The cautious middle ground.
  5. **Malapert Massif** → Best communications, excellent power, poor ice access. The comms-focused play.
- **Consequences**: Landing site determines terrain difficulty (affects crash risk), ice extraction yields (affects life support renewal), solar power (affects ongoing consumption), and communications reliability (affects event severity) for the rest of the game.
- **Can paths reconverge?**: No. Once you land, you stay. The landing site shapes every subsequent turn.

#### Go / No-Go for TLI (Phase 2)

- **When**: After reaching low Earth orbit and completing systems check
- **Context**: The player has launched successfully and is in LEO. Systems check may reveal damage.
- **Options**:
  1. **Go for TLI** → Commit to the Moon. No cheap return.
  2. **Delay in orbit** → Costs 1 turn and 0.5 life support, may improve solar forecast.
  3. **Abort to Earth** → Mission ends. Crew survives. Score: 0.
- **Consequences**: Proceeding with damaged systems creates compounding risk. Aborting preserves the crew but ends the game.

#### Descent Fuel Allocation (Phase 5)

- **When**: Beginning of powered descent
- **Context**: The player chooses how much fuel to spend on the landing burn. More fuel = safer landing but fewer reserves.
- **Options**:
  1. **Full burn (60–80 FU)** → Best landing score, minimal crash risk.
  2. **Moderate burn (50–60 FU)** → Acceptable landing score, preserves some fuel.
  3. **Minimum burn (45–50 FU)** → High crash risk, maximum fuel preservation.
- **Consequences**: Landing quality affects spare parts, shielding, crew health, and life support entering the critical surface phase.

## Milestone Narration Texts

### 1. Mission Briefing (Phase 1 Introduction)

> **ARTEMIS VII — PRE-MISSION BRIEFING**
> **Johnson Space Center, Houston, Texas**
> **Classified: FOUO**
>
> The room is small and windowless — Building 30, Conference Room 4B. The same room where Apollo 13's rescue was planned. The walls are bare except for the NASA meatball logo and a framed photograph of Buzz Aldrin's bootprint. You've been in this room a dozen times. It has never felt this heavy.
>
> NASA Administrator Vasquez stands at the front, a tablet in one hand and the weight of an agency's future on both shoulders. She doesn't smile. She doesn't need to.
>
> "Good morning. You know why you're here. You know what's being asked. But let me say it plainly, because in forty-eight hours you'll be in a spacecraft and I'll be in this room watching your telemetry, and I want you to hear this from me, face to face.
>
> "Artemis VII is not a visit. It is not a flag-planting exercise. It is not a photo opportunity for the administration. You are going to the Moon to stay. You are going to establish the first permanent human presence on another world. If you succeed, everything that follows — Mars, the outer planets, the stars — starts with you. If you fail..."
>
> She pauses. Looks at each of you, one at a time.
>
> "If you fail, we try again. But I would very much prefer you not fail.
>
> "Your budget is fixed. I fought for every dollar and I lost more fights than I won. You won't have everything you want. You'll have to decide what you can't live without — literally. Commander, that's your call. Your crew trusts you. I trust you. Don't make me regret it.
>
> "Dismissed. Go pack your spacecraft. And Commander? Don't forget the duct tape."
>
> Rivera raises a hand. "Ma'am, I packed it last week."
>
> For the first time, Vasquez smiles.

### 2. Launch Countdown (Phase 2 Start)

> **LAUNCH DAY — PAD 39B, KENNEDY SPACE CENTER**
> **T-MINUS 00:00:10**
>
> You are lying on your back, 322 feet above the ground, strapped into a seat that vibrates with the thrum of eight million pounds of thrust waiting to happen. The crew cabin smells like new plastic and recycled oxygen. Through the tiny porthole, you see a slice of blue Florida sky.
>
> Chen's voice is glass-smooth on the loop. "Pilot confirms Go for launch."
>
> Rivera, without looking up from the systems panel: "Engineering is Go. All boards are green. Bessie is ready."
>
> Chen: "Bessie is the engine. You don't get to name the engine."
>
> Rivera: "I already named it. It's in the documentation."
>
> Okafor: "Science is Go. And for the record, I think Bessie is a lovely name."
>
> Park: "Medical is Go. Everyone's heart rate is elevated. That's normal. That's good. It means you're not robots."
>
> **T-MINUS 00:00:10**
>
> The countdown clock hits single digits.
>
> *Ten.*
>
> The main engines ignite. The entire vehicle shudders. You feel it in your teeth.
>
> *Nine. Eight. Seven.*
>
> Turbopumps at full speed. Flame trench boiling.
>
> *Six. Five.*
>
> "Go for SRB ignition," says Launch Control.
>
> *Four. Three. Two. One.*
>
> The solid rocket boosters light. The hold-down bolts blow. Eight million pounds of thrust lifts you off the pad. The acceleration presses you into your seat — three Gs, then four. The cabin shakes like a paint mixer. The noise is everything. You can't hear your own breathing.
>
> Through the comm static, Chen's voice: "Tower clear. Roll program initiated."
>
> You are leaving Earth.
>
> There is no turning back.

---

### 3. Earth in the Rearview (Phase 3 Start — Transit Begins)

> **TRANS-LUNAR INJECTION COMPLETE**
> **MISSION ELAPSED TIME: 00:08:42:17**
>
> The TLI burn is done. The engine cuts off and silence hits like a wall. After eight hours of vibration, alarms, and adrenaline, the sudden quiet is almost worse. You float against your harness. The crew cabin drifts in free fall. The only sound is the hum of the air recyclers and the soft tick of cooling metal.
>
> You look out the porthole.
>
> Earth is behind you now — a blue marble framed in the tiny window, impossibly bright against the black. You can see the whole Atlantic Ocean. You can see the curve of Africa. You can see the swirl of a hurricane tracking north. It is the most beautiful thing you have ever seen, and it is getting smaller.
>
> "Two hundred forty thousand miles to go," Chen says from the pilot station, scrolling through trajectory data. "Three days. Give or take."
>
> Okafor is pressed against the opposite porthole, camera floating beside them. "Commander. You need to see this. That's — that's everything. Everyone we've ever known. Everyone who's ever lived. It's all right there. And it fits in a window."
>
> Rivera looks up from a checklist. "It also fits in a targeting reticle, if you want to be existential about it."
>
> Park: "Rivera, maybe save the existential dread for day two."
>
> Rivera: "That's fair."
>
> You settle in. Three days of coasting through the void. Three days of monitoring systems, rationing supplies, and watching the pale blue dot shrink behind you. Ahead, the Moon waits — grey, silent, and very far away.
>
> Your supplies are finite. The journey has begun.

---

### 4. Gateway Arrival (Phase 4 Start)

> **LUNAR GATEWAY STATION — NEAR-RECTILINEAR HALO ORBIT**
> **MISSION ELAPSED TIME: 03:14:22:08**
>
> The Moon fills your viewport.
>
> You've seen photographs. You've run simulations. You've studied every crater, every rille, every shadow in the south polar region until you could sketch them from memory. None of it prepared you for this.
>
> The Moon is *enormous*. It hangs below you like a frozen ocean — grey and white and utterly still. The terminator line cuts across the surface, sharp as a razor, dividing blinding sunlight from absolute shadow. At the south pole, the shadows are eternal. Craters that haven't seen sunlight in two billion years yawn in the darkness. Somewhere down there, in that ancient night, water ice waits for you.
>
> "Contact with Gateway," Chen reports. "Range: twelve kilometers. Closing at point-four meters per second. Docking alignment is nominal."
>
> Gateway appears ahead — a small, bright cross against the grey. It's smaller than you expected. A collection of modules no bigger than a school bus, each one representing a different nation's investment in the future. The ESA service module. The JAXA habitation ring. The CSA robotic arm, folded like a sleeping mantis. Humanity's farthest outpost.
>
> "Permission to dock, Gateway Control."
>
> A pause. Light-speed delay. Then a voice you don't recognize:
>
> "Artemis VII, Gateway Control. You are cleared to dock at Port 2. Welcome to the neighborhood. Coffee's terrible, but it's hot."
>
> Rivera: "That's the most reassuring thing anyone has said to me in three days."
>
> You dock. The seal pressurizes. The hatch opens. Gateway smells like recycled air and aluminum. Supply racks line the walls. Everything here costs double what it cost at KSC — it had to be launched from Earth months ago.
>
> This is the last outpost. The last fort before the wilderness. Resupply if you can, rest if you must, choose your landing site, and prepare to descend.
>
> Below you, the Moon waits.

---

### 5. Landing Site Selected (Phase 4 End)

> **LANDING SITE CONFIRMED**
> **SITE: [SELECTED SITE NAME]**
>
> The holographic terrain map hovers over the Gateway's planning table, casting blue light across the crew's faces. You've studied the data. You've weighed the trade-offs. And now you've made the call.
>
> You point to the map. "[Site Name]. That's where we're going."
>
> The room is quiet for a beat.
>
> Chen nods slowly, already computing descent trajectories in their head. "I can put us down there. Tight margins on the terrain, but I can do it."
>
> Rivera pulls up a systems schematic. "Landing gear can handle the slope tolerance. Barely. I'll reinforce the shock absorbers during transfer."
>
> Okafor leans in closer to the terrain map, eyes bright. "The ice readings from this site are... Commander, if those deposits are even half of what the orbital scans suggest, we'll have water for years."
>
> Park says nothing for a moment. Then: "I've updated the medical contingency plan for the site-specific hazards. I'd rather not use it."
>
> You transmit the selection to Houston. Light-speed delay. One and a half seconds to Earth. One and a half seconds back. Three seconds of silence that feel like thirty.
>
> Houston responds: "Artemis VII, we copy your landing site selection. Mission Control concurs. You are Go for lander transfer and powered descent. Good luck, Commander. The next time you call us, you'll be standing on the Moon."
>
> You look at your crew. They look back at you. Four people who crossed 240,000 miles on your word.
>
> Time to earn it.

---

### 6. Descent Begins (Phase 5 Start)

> **POWERED DESCENT INITIATION**
> **ALTITUDE: 15,000 METERS**
> **FUEL: [CURRENT FU] FUEL UNITS**
>
> The lander undocks from Gateway with a soft clunk and a hiss of released pressure. Through the triangular windows, the Moon rises to meet you — no longer a landscape below, but a world rushing upward.
>
> "Descent engine primed," Chen reports, hands steady on the attitude controller. "PDI burn in thirty seconds."
>
> Rivera's voice is fast but controlled: "All boards green. Fuel cells nominal. Landing radar online. I've got telemetry on all four legs."
>
> You feel the weight return as the descent engine ignites — a gentle push at first, then building. The lander tips, pitches, and begins its controlled fall toward the surface. Altitude numbers spin downward on the heads-up display.
>
> *12,000 meters.*
>
> *10,000 meters.*
>
> *8,000 meters.*
>
> The terrain below resolves from abstract grey to specific boulders, specific shadows, specific craters. Your landing zone is a patch of flat regolith between hazards, and it's getting bigger fast.
>
> "Throttle up," Chen says, and the engine roars louder.
>
> This is it. Every resource decision, every course correction, every trade-off you've made since Kennedy Space Center — it all comes down to the next sixty seconds.
>
> Park, strapped into the jump seat behind you, murmurs: "No pressure."
>
> Okafor, despite everything, laughs.

---

### 7. Touchdown (Phase 5 Success)

> **CONTACT LIGHT.**
>
> The call comes from Rivera, watching the leg sensors. The lander settles. Dust — grey, ancient, untouched for a billion years — billows outward from the descent engine exhaust in slow, silent arcs. In one-sixth gravity, it hangs in the vacuum like fog.
>
> Chen's hands leave the controls. "Engine stop. Descent complete."
>
> For a moment, nobody moves. The lander creaks. Metal contracts in the lunar cold. The engine ticks as it cools. Through the windows: grey regolith, black sky, and a horizon that curves just slightly wrong — too close, too sharp, too *alien*.
>
> Then Chen speaks, and their voice is the steadiest thing in two worlds:
>
> "Houston, Artemis VII. The Eagle has landed."
>
> A pause. Light-speed delay. The longest one and a half seconds of your life.
>
> Then Houston erupts. You can hear the cheering through the static — not the polished, controlled voices of Mission Control, but the raw, human sound of two hundred engineers who just put five people on the Moon.
>
> "Artemis VII, Houston. We copy you on the surface. You — you are Go for surface operations. Congratulations, Commander. You made it."
>
> Rivera exhales a breath they've been holding since Phase 2. "The landing gear is nominal. All four pads are in contact. No tilt warning. No leaks."
>
> Okafor presses their face against the porthole. Their breath fogs the glass. "I can see the ice field. Commander, I can *see* it. It's right there."
>
> Park unstraps from the jump seat and reaches for the medical kit. "Alright. Vitals check. Everyone. Now. Then we get to work."
>
> You look out the window at the grey world that is now your home.
>
> You made it to the Moon. Now you have to survive it.

---

### 8. First EVA (Phase 6 Start)

> **FIRST EXTRAVEHICULAR ACTIVITY**
> **MISSION ELAPSED TIME: 04:06:18:33**
>
> The airlock cycles. Pressure drops to zero. The outer hatch swings open, and the Moon is right there — not through a window, not on a screen, not in a simulation. Real. Under your boots. As close as the ground has ever been.
>
> You step down the ladder. Each rung is a small act of faith. Your boots crunch on regolith that has never been touched. The dust is fine as talcum powder, dark grey, and it clings to everything — your gloves, your visor, the folds of your suit. It smells, impossibly, like spent gunpowder. Nobody knows why.
>
> The horizon is close. Disturbingly close. On Earth, the horizon is fifteen miles away. Here, it's less than two. The world curves away in every direction, and beyond the edge: stars. More stars than you've ever seen. The Milky Way is a river of light from one side of the sky to the other. And there, hanging in the black, impossibly bright, impossibly blue — Earth.
>
> You are standing on the Moon, and Earth is the size of a marble in the sky.
>
> "Commander, comms check." Chen's voice in your helmet. "How's the view?"
>
> You try to answer. Your throat is tight. Not from fear. Not from the suit. From the weight of the moment — a weight that has nothing to do with gravity.
>
> "It's..." You clear your throat. "It's worth the trip."
>
> Behind you, Rivera is already unloading equipment from the lander, muttering about dust infiltration rates. Okafor has knelt down and is running their gloved fingers through the regolith with the reverence of a priest at an altar. Park is setting up the emergency medical station with the efficiency of someone who has practiced this exact sequence four hundred times.
>
> You plant your boots. You look at the crater rim. You look at the ice field. You look at the patch of flat regolith where your habitat will stand.
>
> Time to build a colony.

---

### 9. Base Camp Established (Phase 6 Milestone)

> **HABITAT MODULE ALPHA — PRESSURIZED**
> **INTERNAL ATMOSPHERE: NOMINAL**
> **MISSION ELAPSED TIME: 04:11:03:22**
>
> The habitat inflates with a sound like a slow exhale — a long, sighing hiss as compressed air fills the rigid shell. The walls stiffen. The pressure gauge climbs. Rivera watches it like a hawk watching a mouse, one hand on the emergency seal valve, the other gripping their checklist notebook hard enough to dent the cover.
>
> "Point-eight atmospheres. Point-nine. One-point-zero. Holding." Rivera's voice cracks on the last word. "It's holding. The seal is good. The habitat is pressurized."
>
> For the first time since launch, you hear a sound that isn't filtered through a helmet speaker. The gentle whisper of air circulation inside a room. A room on the Moon.
>
> The habitat is fifteen feet across. It smells like new plastic and metal sealant. The floor is grey regolith covered by an anti-dust mat. There are four fold-down bunks, a compact galley station, a waste management unit that everyone is politely pretending doesn't exist, and a single round window looking out at the lunar surface.
>
> Park steps inside, unclips their helmet, and takes a breath of unfiltered habitat air. "Not bad. Smells like a new car. A new car in a parking lot with no atmosphere. But not bad."
>
> Okafor follows, and immediately tapes a printed photograph of a sunset over the Indian Ocean to the wall above their bunk. "Home away from home."
>
> Chen enters last, looking back over their shoulder at the lander, the equipment, the bootprints radiating across the grey dust. "Commander. We built a house on the Moon."
>
> You pull the hatch closed. The seal engages. The habitat hums.
>
> For the first time in five days, you eat a meal at a table instead of squeezing paste from a tube while floating. The food is the same freeze-dried ration packs, but sitting down to eat them — with gravity, with walls, with crew — makes them taste like a feast.
>
> Rivera raises a pouch of reconstituted coffee. "To Habitat Module Alpha. May the seals hold and the plumbing work."
>
> You raise yours. "To the crew. We got here."
>
> The habitat hums. The Moon is quiet outside. And for the first time since launch, the mission feels less like survival and more like living.

## Endings

### Victory Endings

---

#### 1. Thriving Colony (Best Ending)

**Condition**: 4+ crew surviving, high resources remaining, science objectives completed.

> **COLONY ASSESSMENT: THRIVING**
> **FINAL SCORE: [SCORE]**
>
> **=== MISSION COMPLETE ===**
>
> You stand on the crater rim at dawn — or what passes for dawn at the south pole, where the Sun creeps along the horizon like a searchlight sweeping a prison yard, never fully rising, never fully setting. The habitat complex is behind you: three pressurized modules, solar arrays gleaming, the ISRU processor humming as it converts ice to water and oxygen. Bootprints radiate across the regolith like the spokes of a wheel, extending farther each day.
>
> Your crew is alive. All of them. Every specialist who climbed into that spacecraft at Kennedy Space Center is standing on the Moon, working, building, *living*.
>
> You open the comm channel to Earth.
>
> "Houston, Artemis VII. Commander's final mission report.
>
> "Colony site is established and self-sustaining. Habitat is pressurized, ISRU is online, water extraction is exceeding projections. Solar power is nominal. Crew health is good — all five members active and operational. Dr. Okafor reports the ice deposits are consistent with orbital surveys. Specialist Rivera reports all critical systems are redundant. Dr. Park reports zero untreated medical conditions. Dr. Chen is already planning a rover traverse to the secondary crater.
>
> "Houston, we have a colony."
>
> The silence stretches. Light-speed delay.
>
> Then, a new voice. Not Mission Control. Not the flight director.
>
> "Commander, this is the President of the United States. On behalf of the American people, and on behalf of every nation that contributed to this mission — I want to tell you something that I've been waiting to say since you launched.
>
> "Welcome home. Not home to Earth. Home to the Moon. Because that's what you've made it. A home. The first home humanity has ever built beyond the world that built us.
>
> "What you've done — what your crew has done — will be remembered as long as human beings look up at the night sky. You didn't just land on the Moon. You stayed. You built. You *lived*.
>
> "We're already naming the colony. Mission Control is calling it [Player's Colony Name]. I think that's fitting.
>

## Recurring Narrative Elements

### Motifs

#### "The Pale Blue Dot"

Earth, visible through the porthole, getting smaller. It appears in transit narration, in moments of crisis, in crew dialogue, and in the endings. It's the visual anchor for the game's central emotional truth: you are far from home, and home is getting farther. Early in the game, looking at Earth is a comfort. By mid-transit, it's a reminder of distance. On the surface, it's a symbol — either of what you're building toward (return) or what you've left behind (sacrifice).

- **Phase 2**: Earth fills the viewport. Beautiful, overwhelming, *close*.
- **Phase 3**: Earth shrinks. Fits in a porthole. Fits in a fist held at arm's length.
- **Phase 4**: Earth is a bright marble in the black. You can cover it with your thumb.
- **Phase 6**: Earth hangs low on the lunar horizon, half-lit. It looks fragile. It looks like something worth protecting.
- **Endings**: Earth is the last thing the commander sees in every ending — triumph or tragedy.

#### "One More Turn"

Explicitly invoked in the turn-based structure. The game's addictive quality is baked into the pacing — each turn is short enough that "one more" feels harmless, but the cumulative effect of many turns is where the tension builds. Crew members occasionally reference this:

- Rivera: *"One more diagnostic, then I'll sleep."* (They don't sleep.)
- Okafor: *"One more sample. Just one more."*
- Park: *"One more day. We can make it one more day."*

#### Silence

The Moon has no atmosphere. No sound carries outside the habitat. This silence is referenced repeatedly — it's alien, unsettling, and eventually becomes a kind of companion. The game uses it in moments of transition and loss.

### Running Jokes / Callbacks

#### Mission Control's Coffee

Every interaction with Houston or Gateway Control includes a reference to coffee — its quality, its availability, its temperature, or its absence. This is a running thread through the entire game.

- **Phase 1** (Briefing): Administrator Vasquez has a coffee cup with "Per Aspera Ad Astra" on it.
- **Phase 3** (Transit): Mission Control sign-off: *"Get some rest, Artemis VII. The coffee here is keeping us awake for you."*
- **Phase 4** (Gateway): Gateway Control: *"Coffee's terrible, but it's hot."*
- **Phase 6** (Surface): After base camp is established, Rivera jury-rigs a water heater. *"It's not coffee. It's hot water and wishful thinking. But it's the best hot water and wishful thinking on the Moon."*
- **Endings** (Thriving): Final Houston transmission: *"Commander, when the relief crew arrives, they're bringing real coffee. Columbian. The good stuff. You've earned it."*

#### Rivera's Duct Tape Solutions

Rivera's engineering philosophy involves an almost spiritual reverence for duct tape, WD-40, and percussive maintenance. This becomes a running joke across the mission.

- **Phase 1**: Rivera packs extra duct tape. Administrator Vasquez approves.
- **Phase 3**: *Rivera patches a micrometeorite hole with... duct tape. "It's rated for vacuum," they say defensively. "I checked."*
- **Phase 5**: During a tense descent, Rivera's solution to a sensor glitch: *"I can fix it. I need thirty seconds and — yes — duct tape."*
- **Phase 6**: Rivera's surface EVA repair kit is 40% duct tape by volume. *"Paper doesn't glitch. Tape doesn't lie."*

#### "At Least It's Not Dysentery"

The Oregon Trail callback. Appears exactly three times in the game, escalating each time:

1. **Phase 3** (Transit — food contamination event): *Park examines the contaminated rations. "Bacterial growth. We need to jettison these before it spreads." Pause. "At least it's not dysentery."*
2. **Phase 6** (Surface — crew illness event): *A crew member reports gastrointestinal distress from mineral-contaminated water. Park: "Lunar dysentery. I can't believe I'm saying 'lunar dysentery.' Actually, I can. At least it's not... well. It is. It's dysentery. On the Moon."*
3. **Funeral Sequence** (Death option C): *"At least it wasn't dysentery."*

#### Equipment Manual Jokes

Okafor and Rivera have a running bit about the absurd length of equipment manuals.

- *"Step 47 of 312. 'Rotate the locking collar counterclockwise.' Which way is counterclockwise in one-sixth gravity?"*
- *"The ISRU manual is fourteen hundred pages. Okafor read the whole thing. On purpose."*
- *"Page 847: 'In the event of a catastrophic depressurization, consult page 1,203.' That's not helpful. That's a scavenger hunt."*

### Foreshadowing

#### Early Warning Signs

- **Phase 1** (Mission Prep): If the player under-allocates shielding, Rivera comments: *"Commander, the solar forecast for our transit window is... active. Are you sure about the shielding allocation?"* This hints at potential solar events in Phase 3.
- **Phase 2** (Launch): A brief mention of "Kessler cascade debris in cislunar space" in the pre-launch briefing foreshadows the Space Debris Field event in Phase 3.
- **Phase 3** (Transit): Okafor mentions that the ice deposit data from orbital surveys "might be optimistic." This foreshadows potential lower-than-expected yields on the surface.

#### Crew Conversations That Set Up Character Arcs

- **Chen and Rivera** (Phase 3): A quiet conversation about whether Chen will fly again after this mission. Chen is evasive. This sets up Chen's arc — the pilot who might be making their last flight, and who cares more about getting the crew down safely than about their own future.
- **Okafor and Park** (Phase 3): Okafor asks Park what they'd do if they found something unexpected on the surface — something that changed what we know about the Moon. Park: *"I'd make sure you lived long enough to publish it."* This sets up both characters' motivations: Okafor's discovery drive, Park's protective instinct.
- **Rivera** (Phase 2): Rivera's pre-launch checklist obsession — played for humor early — becomes genuinely critical when Rivera's diligence catches a real fault. The joke becomes the save.

## Dialogue & Writing Style

### Voice

- **Narrator voice**: Second person, present tense. *"You check the instrument panel. The oxygen readout flickers — 78%, and dropping."* The narrator is omniscient but not chatty. They describe what the player sees, hears, and feels, but they don't editorialize. The voice is clipped, precise, almost military — the voice of a mission report written in real time.
- **NPC voice**: First person, distinct personality per character. Each crew member has a recognizable speech pattern:
  - **Chen**: Short sentences. Minimal emotion. Dry observations. *"Trajectory is clean. Two percent under budget."*
  - **Rivera**: Longer, more anxious phrasing. Self-corrections. Nervous humor. *"Okay, okay, the seal is good. I think it's good. It's good. Probably."*
  - **Okafor**: Enthusiastic, detailed, peppered with scientific references. Warm. *"Commander, the ice concentration here is even higher than my models predicted!"*
  - **Park**: Calm, wry, medically precise. Dark humor. *"In one-sixth gravity, your broken leg only hurts one-sixth as much. The bad news is, that's not how pain works."*
  - **Mission Control**: Professional with occasional humanity bleeding through. *"Copy, Artemis VII. And, uh — great job up there. Unofficially."*
- **Vocabulary level**: Technically informed but accessible. Space jargon is used when it feels natural (*TLI*, *EVA*, *ISRU*, *PDI*) but always in context that makes the meaning clear. No glossary needed.

### Dialogue Conventions

- NPC speech is formatted in italics within narrative text: *Chen adjusts the trajectory. "Burn complete. We're on profile."*
- Crew dialogue during events is integrated into the event narration, not separated into dialogue boxes.
- Average dialogue exchange: 2–4 lines. Crew members speak briefly — they're professionals in a crisis, not characters in a play. When someone speaks at length, it means something is wrong (or very right).
- The Commander (player) rarely has scripted dialogue — their "voice" is their decisions. When the commander does speak in narrated scenes, it's kept minimal to preserve player projection.

### Example Passages

#### 1. A Tense Moment

> The solar flare warning comes at 03:14 mission time, when half the crew is asleep.
>

>

>
> You have two.
>

>
> The alarm pulses. The numbers climb.
>
> Decide.

>

#### 3. A Triumphant Moment

> The drill bites into the regolith and hits ice at two meters.

We can make *water* from this."

## Cultural Sensitivity

### Representation Guidelines

- **Diverse crew by design**: The crew of Artemis VII reflects the international, multiethnic reality of modern space programs. Dr. Chen, Specialist Rivera, Dr. Okafor, and Dr. Park represent different cultural backgrounds, and this is treated as unremarkable — because in the Artemis era, it *is* unremarkable. No crew member's heritage is the subject of tokenism, jokes, or special narrative focus. They are astronauts first.
- **International cooperation**: Lunar Gateway includes modules from ESA (European Space Agency), JAXA (Japan Aerospace Exploration Agency), and CSA (Canadian Space Agency). International partners are referenced respectfully and portrayed as essential contributors, not sideline players. The game acknowledges that space exploration is a human achievement, not a national one — even when funded nationally.
- **Gender neutrality**: All crew members use gender-neutral pronouns (they/them) in narrative text. The player's commander is not assigned a gender. This is both inclusive and practical — it avoids assumptions and allows every player to project themselves into the role.
- **Avoiding stereotypes**: No crew member is reduced to their cultural background. Chen is not "the quiet Asian pilot" — they're a specific person who happens to be quiet and precise. Rivera is not "the anxious Latino engineer" — they're a specific person who happens to be meticulous. Characterization comes from personality, not identity.
- **Historical harm**: The game does not reference colonialism, exploitation, or displacement of indigenous peoples in the context of "colonizing" the Moon. The word "colony" is used in its scientific/space-program sense (a permanent settlement), not its historical-imperial sense. The Moon has no inhabitants to displace.

### Content Warnings

- **Crew death**: Characters the player has spent the entire game with can die. These deaths are brief but emotionally weighted. The game does not linger on graphic detail, but it does not shy away from loss.
- **Isolation and confinement**: The game's setting — a small spacecraft 240,000 miles from Earth — inherently involves themes of isolation, claustrophobia, and helplessness. These are handled with care and counterbalanced by crew camaraderie and humor.
- **Dark humor around death**: The "Funeral Sequence" (Congressional Debrief) uses humor to process failure and death, in the Oregon Trail tradition. This humor is absurd and institutional, never cruel. It laughs at bureaucracy and human coping mechanisms, not at the dead.

## Player Experience — Emotional Arc

The intended emotional journey across all 7 phases:

```
Phase 1: Mission Prep
├── Emotion: EXCITEMENT + ANXIETY
├── "So many choices, not enough budget"
├── The thrill of preparing for something historic
└── The dawning realization that you can't take everything

Phase 2: Launch & Earth Orbit
├── Emotion: TERROR + EXHILARATION
├── "The rocket is shaking and I am on top of it"
├── The rush of launch, the beauty of orbit
└── The Go/No-Go decision — commit or abort

Phase 3: Lunar Transit
├── Emotion: WATCHFULNESS + CREEPING DREAD
├── "Everything is fine. Why am I so nervous?"
├── Monitoring resources, dreading the event roll
├── Earth shrinking, the void pressing in
└── Crew banter provides relief; silence amplifies tension

Phase 4: Lunar Orbit & Gateway
├── Emotion: AWE + STRATEGIC FOCUS
├── "The Moon is RIGHT THERE"
├── First close-up view of the destination
├── The shopkeeper's calculus at Gateway
└── Landing site selection — the biggest strategic bet

Phase 5: Descent & Landing
├── Emotion: WHITE-KNUCKLE TENSION
├── "Everything depends on the next sixty seconds"
├── All prior decisions come due
├── The altitude numbers spinning down
└── Touchdown — catharsis or catastrophe

Phase 6: Surface Operations
├── Emotion: GRITTY DETERMINATION + WONDER
├── "We made it. Now we have to survive."
├── The awe of first EVA, the grind of daily survival
├── Equipment failures, dust, cold, radiation
├── Building something permanent with diminishing resources
└── Each EVA is a small victory; each failure is a wound

Phase 7: Colony Establishment
├── Emotion: TRIUMPH or TRAGEDY or BITTERSWEET ACCEPTANCE
├── "Did we make it?"
├── The final tally — crew, resources, objectives
├── The last message to Earth
└── The weight of what it cost, balanced against what it built
```

**The overall arc**: Hope → Commitment → Endurance → Crisis → Resolution.

The game should feel like the player *earned* their ending — whether it's the triumph of a thriving colony, the bittersweet survival of a diminished crew, or the dark comedy of a Congressional debrief for a mission that went catastrophically wrong.

The best outcome feels hard-won. The worst outcome feels like it could have gone differently — if only you'd packed more shielding, or taken the rest turn, or picked a different landing site. That "if only" is the engine of replayability.

## Implementation Notes

- **Text storage**: All narrative text in this document should be stored as plain-text templates with variable interpolation markers (e.g., `[CREW_NAME]`, `[SITE_NAME]`, `[SCORE]`). Templates are organized by phase and event type.
- **Branching**: The narrative does not branch heavily — the same milestone texts play regardless of path, with variable substitution for context-specific details (landing site name, surviving crew, resource levels). Endings are selected based on final game state, not accumulated narrative flags.
- **Text length**: Milestone narratives target 200–400 words. Event narratives target 50–150 words. Status descriptions target 20–50 words. Crew death memorials target 50–100 words. Endings target 300–500 words.
- **Dynamic text**: Crew dialogue in events should reference only living crew members. If a crew member referenced in a template is dead, the template falls back to a generic version or omits the line.
- **Localization**: All narrative text should avoid idioms that don't translate well. Technical space terminology (EVA, TLI, ISRU) is internationally consistent and doesn't require localization. Cultural references (Oregon Trail callbacks, Congressional hearing) may need adaptation for non-US audiences.
- **Pacing**: Text should appear with brief delays between paragraphs in milestone sequences (500ms–1s per paragraph) to create dramatic pacing. Event text displays instantly. The player should never wait more than 3 seconds for text to appear.
